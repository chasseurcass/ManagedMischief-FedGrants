# Residential Substance Abuse Treatment For State Prisoners Program [ALN # 16.593]

Introduction: What is an RSAT Program?

A Residential Substance Abuse Treatment (RSAT) program is a specialized, dedicated program within a correctional facility designed to help individuals with substance use disorders. Its core purpose is to provide intensive treatment that prepares these individuals for successful reintegration into their communities after release. These programs are not arbitrary; as detailed in the Promising Practices Guidelines for Residential Substance Abuse Treatment upon which this guide is based, they are built upon specific requirements and therapeutic models, such as the Therapeutic Community, which research has shown to be effective in reducing relapse and reoffending. To understand how these programs achieve their goals, it is essential to first look at who they are for and the careful process used for admission.

1. The First Step: Intake, Screening, and Assessment

Getting the right people into the program is the critical first step for any successful RSAT program. This is achieved through a multi-stage process of intake, screening, and comprehensive assessment to ensure that treatment is directed where it will be most effective.

The intake process is guided by three essential principles:

* Eligibility Criteria: Admission isn't random; it's primarily based on evidence-based assessments. A candidate should have a diagnosed moderate to severe substance use disorder and be identified as having a medium to high criminogenic risk, meaning they have a significant likelihood of reoffending without intervention.
* Screening for Co-Occurring Disorders: Substance use is often intertwined with other challenges. Programs must screen for mental health issues, including trauma and mental illness, because these co-occurring disorders can significantly impact a person's recovery and must be addressed as part of a holistic treatment plan.
* Individualized Treatment Plans: Every person's journey is unique. A comprehensive "biopsychosocial assessment" is conducted to understand each participant's specific needs, covering their physical health, mental state, and social situation. This assessment becomes the foundation for a tailored treatment plan that guides their recovery.

Research suggests that substance use disorder treatment does not need to be voluntary to be effective.

Now that we understand the 'who' and 'why' of admission, let's explore the 'what'—the fundamental structure and components that define an RSAT program.

2. The Program Blueprint: Core Components and Structure

Effective RSAT programs are not just a series of counseling sessions; they are highly structured, immersive environments designed to facilitate profound personal change. This structure is built on a foundation of legally mandated components and a proven therapeutic model.

The federal statute authorizing RSAT funding requires programs to include several key structural elements:

Required Component	Why It's Important
Minimum Duration	Prison-based programs must last at least six months, and jail-based programs at least three. This provides the necessary time for participants to engage in meaningful and lasting therapeutic change.
Physical Separation	Participants are housed in a unit separate from the general population, often called a "treatment pod." This creates a safe, focused, and supportive environment free from negative influences.
Mandatory Drug Testing	Participants are tested for illicit substances upon admission and randomly throughout the program. This ensures a drug-free environment and provides opportunities for therapeutic intervention if a relapse occurs.
Prioritizing Aftercare Services	Programs are required to prioritize providing aftercare services. This acknowledges that recovery is a long-term process that continues well beyond the period of incarceration.

The foundational model for most RSAT programs is the Therapeutic Community (TC). In a TC, the community itself—composed of peers and staff working together—is the primary instrument for change. This group-based approach fosters accountability, teaches pro-social skills, and helps participants develop a new lifestyle centered on honesty, responsibility, and mutual support.

With this blueprint in place, the program can then deploy specific therapeutic tools to help participants rebuild their lives.

3. The Tools for Change: Treatment and Service Interventions

Within the structured environment of an RSAT program, specific evidence-based therapies are used to help participants change the thinking and behaviors that fuel substance use and criminal activity.

Two of the most prominent and effective therapeutic approaches are:

* Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): This approach helps participants identify, challenge, and change the dysfunctional thoughts, beliefs, and patterns of behavior that lead to substance use. By understanding the connection between their thoughts and actions, individuals can develop new coping skills and make healthier choices. This clinical therapy is often complemented by structured Cognitive Behavioral Interventions (CBIs)—such as the 'Thinking for a Change' curriculum—which can be delivered by well-trained program staff to build specific pro-social skills.
* Motivational Interviewing: This is a client-centered, collaborative counseling style used to strengthen a person's own motivation and commitment to change. It is particularly effective for individuals who may feel ambivalent or resistant to treatment, helping them resolve their uncertainty and embrace recovery.

Beyond these core therapies, RSAT programs provide a range of other services to address the whole person and prepare them for a successful life after release.

Essential Support Services

* Mental health and trauma-informed services
* Vocational and educational services
* Housing assistance planning
* Family and social support counseling
* Peer recovery support

A modern standard of care for opioid and alcohol use disorders is Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT). This involves the use of FDA-approved medications, such as naltrexone or buprenorphine, in combination with counseling and behavioral therapies to treat substance use disorders.

Ultimately, the skills and insights gained during treatment must be tested and supported in the real world. This is why a successful recovery depends on a carefully planned journey back into the community.

4. The Path Forward: Transition and Aftercare Planning

The time spent in an RSAT program is only the first phase of treatment. True, lasting recovery depends on continuity of care—a seamless transition from the structured environment of the correctional facility to ongoing support in the community. Without this connection, even the most significant gains can be lost.

People who complete prison-based treatment and continue with treatment in the community typically show the best outcomes.

Effective aftercare is not an afterthought; it is a core part of the RSAT model that begins long before release. The process involves three essential elements:

1. Prerelease Planning: This process starts well before an individual's release date. It involves close collaboration between RSAT staff, community corrections officers (such as probation or parole agents), and community-based treatment providers to create a detailed, individualized reentry plan.
2. A "Warm Handoff": This means actively connecting the individual to their aftercare providers and services before they are released. Staff will schedule initial appointments, ensure records are transferred, and help enroll the person in Medicaid or other health insurance to guarantee there is no gap in care.
3. Community Support Linkages: A robust aftercare plan connects the individual to a full spectrum of support. This goes beyond just treatment and includes stable housing, employment opportunities, medical and dental care, and connections to positive social networks like peer support groups.

With all these components in place, the final question is: how do we know if these efforts are actually working?

5. Measuring Success: How We Know RSAT Programs Work

To ensure accountability and effectiveness, RSAT programs must be committed to measuring their results. This involves tracking two distinct but related types of data: program outputs and program outcomes.

Measurement Type	Definition & Example
Program Outputs	These are measures of what the program does and the services it delivers. For example, the number of participants who enroll and complete the program or the total number of counseling sessions provided.
Program Outcomes	These are measures of the program's real-world impact on participants after they are released. For example, reduced rates of rearrest and reincarceration, longer periods of abstinence, or lower rates of overdose.

While outputs are important for monitoring program operations, the most critical measures of success are outcomes. Reduced recidivism (reoffending) and relapse are the ultimate indicators of an RSAT program's effectiveness, demonstrating its ability to help individuals build safer, healthier, and more productive lives in the community.

Conclusion: Key Principles of Effective Treatment

The journey through a Residential Substance Abuse Treatment program is a structured, intensive, and transformative process. It begins with a careful assessment to ensure the right individuals are brought into a safe and supportive environment. Inside, they are equipped with evidence-based tools to change their thinking and behavior, all while preparing for the crucial transition back to the community. This entire process is built on a set of core principles that define effective treatment.

Ultimately, the success of RSAT programs can be distilled into 3 Key Pillars:

1. Targeted Intake and Individualized Planning: Getting the right people into the program and creating a personalized treatment plan that addresses their unique needs and risks.
2. Structured, Evidence-Based Treatment: Using proven methods like Therapeutic Communities and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy within a safe, drug-free, and physically separate environment.
3. Comprehensive Aftercare and Community Connection: Ensuring a seamless "warm handoff" to continued support, treatment, and pro-social resources in the community after release.

GRANT OVERVIEW:
The Residential Substance Abuse Treatment for State Prisoners (RSAT) Formula Grant Program (42 U.S.C. 3796ff et seq.) assists states and units of local government in developing and implementing residential substance abuse treatment programs within state and local correctional and detention facilities in which prisoners are incarcerated for a period of time sufficient to permit substance abuse treatment.

RSAT funds may be used to execute three types of programs: residential, jail-based, and aftercare:

Residential

Last at least 6 and no more than 12 months.
Provide in residential treatment facilities set apart-in a completely separate facility or dedicated housing unit in a facility exclusively for use by RSAT participants-from the general correctional population.
Focus on the substance abuse problems of the inmate.
Develop the inmate's cognitive, behavioral, social, vocational, and other skills to solve the substance abuse and related problems.
Begin or continue requiring urinalysis and/or other proven reliable forms of drug and alcohol testing for program participants and former participants while they remain in the custody of the state or local government.
If possible, RSAT participation should be limited to inmates with 6 to 12 months remaining in their confinement so they can be released from prison instead of returning to the general prison population after completing the program.
Jails & Local Correctional Facilities

Last at least 3 months.
Strive to separate the treatment population from the general correctional population.
Focus on the substance abuse problems of the inmate.
Develop the inmate's cognitive, behavioral, social, vocational, and other skills to solve the substance abuse and related problems.
Design based on effective, scientific practices.
Aftercare
Aftercare services must involve coordination between the correctional treatment program and other social service and rehabilitation programs, such as education and job training, parole supervision, halfway houses, self-help, and peer group programs. To qualify as an aftercare program, the head of the substance abuse treatment program must work in conjunction with state and local authorities and organizations involved in substance abuse treatment to assist in the placement of program participants into community substance abuse treatment facilities on release.

CJCC PRIORITIES/MAIN OBJECTIVES:
The main objective of the RSAT Program is to break the cycle of drugs and violence by reducing the demand for, use, and trafficking of illegal drugs. RSAT enhances the capability of states and units of local and tribal government to:

Provide residential substance abuse treatment for incarcerated inmates;
Prepare offenders for their reintegration into the communities from which they came by incorporating reentry planning activities into treatment programs;
Assist offenders and their communities through the reentry process through the delivery of community-based treatment and other broad-based aftercare services.




# Juvenile SUD Drivers & Drug-Use Situations

(AKA: the SUD-Specific Behavioral Domains DOJ implicitly expects treatment to target)**

If a program’s intervention **directly addresses items in these domains**, it counts as SUD treatment.
If it doesn’t, it is NOT SUD treatment.

## DOMAIN 1. **Internal Emotional Drivers**

These are emotional states that trigger use.

### SUD-specific issues:

- [ ] Using substances to manage anger
- [ ] Using substances to calm anxiety
- [ ] Using substances to cope with depression
- [ ] Using substances after trauma reminders
- [ ] Using substances to avoid emotional discomfort
- [ ] Using substances to feel numb or escape

### What treatment must do:

- [ ] Identify emotional triggers for drug use
- [ ] Teach alternative coping strategies
- [ ] Practice emotional regulation skills *specifically in place of using*


# DOMAIN 2. **Cognitive / Thinking Patterns**

Thoughts that support or justify substance use.

### SUD-specific thinking patterns:

- [ ] “It’s not a big deal, everyone smokes weed.”
- [ ] “I need it to sleep / calm down / focus.”
- [ ] “I can stop any time.”
- [ ] “I only use when I’m mad, so it’s not a problem.”
- [ ] “Probation won’t catch me.”
- [ ] “I won’t get addicted because I’m young.”

### What treatment must do:

- [ ] Identify thoughts tied to drug use
- [ ] Challenge beliefs that reinforce use
- [ ] Replace them with healthier cognitions
- [ ] Build refusal and decision-making skills


## DOMAIN 3. **Behavioral Triggers & Routines**

Specific actions or patterns that precede use.

### SUD-specific behaviors:

- [ ] Using after school
- [ ] Using before/after probation meetings
- [ ] Using when alone and bored
- [ ] Using when certain music, games, or routines cue it
- [ ] Using immediately after fights or discipline
- [ ] Using on weekends with no supervision

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Map the behavior chain leading to use
- [ ] Identify high-risk routines
- [ ] Build replacement behaviors
- [ ] Practice coping in these exact situations


## DOMAIN 4. **Peer Influence Dynamics**

Youth substance use is overwhelmingly social.

### SUD-specific peer drivers:

- [ ] Friends who use
- [ ] Friends who supply substances
- [ ] Peer pressure
- [ ] Using substances to fit in
- [ ] Gang-involved peers who normalize drug use
- [ ] Dating partners who use

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Identify high-risk peers
- [ ] Build peer refusal skills
- [ ] Set up monitoring/limits around peer contact
- [ ] Replace high-risk peer networks with pro-social ones

## DOMAIN 5. **Family & Home Environment**

Home conditions that allow or encourage use.

### SUD-specific family factors:

- [ ] Lack of supervision or inconsistent supervision
- [ ] Family conflict that triggers use
- [ ] Adults in the home who use substances
- [ ] Family norms that minimize substance use
- [ ] No structure/rules around curfew, internet, location

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Increase caregiver monitoring and supervision
- [ ] Improve communication about substance use
- [ ] Create rules and expectations around drug use
- [ ] Strengthen family support and consequences
- [ ] Ensure caregivers can identify relapse warning signs

**THIS is why family therapy is often *essential*, not “supportive.”**
Because these factors DRIVE the substance use.

## DOMAIN 6. **Psychiatric/Co-occurring Symptoms**

Symptoms that directly precipitate drug use.

### SUD-specific co-occurring issues:

- [ ] Trauma
- [ ] PTSD symptoms
- [ ] Depression
- [ ] Anxiety
- [ ] ADHD impulsivity
- [ ] Conduct disorder
- [ ] Emotional dysregulation

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Identify psychiatric triggers
- [ ] Teach coping skills *specifically relevant to drug use moments*
- [ ] Integrate SUD work into MH treatment (not separate)

## DOMAIN 7. **Motivation & Ambivalence**

Youth rarely want to stop using.

### SUD-specific motivational issues:

- [ ] Low motivation to reduce use
- [ ] Not seeing use as harmful
- [ ] Thinking they can hide use from probation
- [ ] Ambivalence about quitting
- [ ] Not linking drug use to consequences

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Use MI strategies to increase motivation to change
- [ ] Create clear change goals around substance use
- [ ] Elicit “change talk” specifically about reducing drugs

## DOMAIN 8. **Relapse Patterns**

Youth relapse for reasons adults often don’t.

### Juvenile relapse drivers:

- [ ] Getting in trouble at school
- [ ] Sports suspension or activity loss
- [ ] Relationship breakups
- [ ] Losing privileges at home
- [ ] Boredom
- [ ] Social media triggers
- [ ] Seasonal/holiday patterns
- [ ] Probation stress

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Create a relapse prevention plan
- [ ] Identify early warning signs
- [ ] Practice strategies for high-risk relapse situations

## DOMAIN 9. **Consequences & Accountability**

Youth often do not connect use with outcomes.

### SUD-specific accountability gaps:

- [ ] Minimal insight into legal or health consequences
- [ ] Not understanding probation requirements
- [ ] Believing they can “game” UA schedules
- [ ] Thinking vaping THC isn’t real use

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Build insight into consequences
- [ ] Link behaviors to probation/court outcomes
- [ ] Teach accountability for drug-related behaviors


## DOMAIN 10. **Skill Deficits**

Youth lack the skills needed to avoid use.

### SUD-specific skill deficits:

- [ ] Refusal skills
- [ ] Planning ahead to avoid high-risk situations
- [ ] Communicating needs to caregivers
- [ ] Managing cravings
- [ ] Problem-solving without substances
- [ ] Conflict resolution that doesn’t escalate into use

### Treatment must:

- [ ] Teach and practice these skills repeatedly
- [ ] Apply them to real drug-use situations, not hypotheticals
---


## If an RSAT applicant can show:

* They identify the youth’s drug-use situations (from the list above)
* They address AT LEAST 4–6 domains with structured intervention
* They provide SUD-specific skill-building
* They set SUD-specific goals
* They measure SUD outcomes

Then they are delivering juvenile SUD treatment.
