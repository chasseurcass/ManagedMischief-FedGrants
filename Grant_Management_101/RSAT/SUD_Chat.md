

# 1. What does **adolescent substance use disorder** *look like?*

SUD in adolescents is **not** the same as adult addiction.
It typically presents as:

* **Behaviorally-driven** use (anger, defiance, identity formation)
* **Emotionally-driven** use (anxiety, depression, trauma)
* **Environmentally-driven** use (peer pressure, lack of supervision)
* **Developmentally-driven** risk-taking
* **Skill deficits** (no coping strategies, poor decision-making)

The “disorder” in adolescents is about **patterns of behavior**, not physiological dependence.

So treating adolescent SUD is not about detox or medication.
It’s about undoing the *behavioral engine* that produces the substance use.

This is the KEY point that unlocks everything else.

---

# 2. So what makes something an *actual* juvenile SUD treatment?

To be SUD treatment, the intervention must include **specific content** that targets **substance use behaviors**.

Not just emotions.
Not just participation.
Not just family dynamics.

**Actual SUD treatment = ordinary therapeutic tools PLUS SUD-specific content.**

The SUD-specific content is the part you’ve never been shown.
So let’s show it.

---

# 3. What makes CBT a *SUD-specific* CBT?

Regular CBT = thoughts, feelings, behaviors.

CBT-for-SUD =
CBT applied to **drug use situations**, including:

* Identifying personal substance-use triggers
* Mapping out the chain: thoughts → feelings → drug use
* Practicing refusal skills
* Learning alternative coping strategies for cravings
* Identifying high-risk people/places
* Building relapse prevention plans
* Tracking use patterns and consequences
* Challenging beliefs that support drug use (“I need weed to sleep”)

**The difference is not the METHOD — it’s the TARGET.**

CBT becomes SUD treatment when it is applied **specifically** to the youth’s substance use.

---

# 4. What makes MI a *SUD-specific* MI?

Regular MI = helping someone explore ambivalence about changing anything.

MI-for-SUD =
MI applied to **substance use behavior**, including:

* Exploring ambivalence about quitting or reducing substance use
* Connecting substance use to the youth’s goals (school, probation, sports, family)
* Identifying reasons for change *specifically* related to the drug use
* Rolling with resistance around sobriety
* Building a commitment plan for reducing use
* Eliciting “change talk” about substance use
* Addressing peer pressure directly

**MI becomes SUD treatment when the entire conversation is anchored to substance use.**

---

# 5. What makes family therapy a *SUD-specific* family therapy?

Regular family therapy = improving relationships, communication, conflict.

SUD-family therapy =
Family therapy applied to reducing drug use:

* Parents learn monitoring strategies for drug-related behavior
* Families create structure to reduce access to substances
* Communication patterns related to arguments about drug use are changed
* Parents learn how to interrupt the youth’s high-risk patterns
* Family develops a relapse prevention plan
* Caregivers learn how peers, boredom, or emotions trigger use
* Consequences and rewards specific to substance use behaviors are established

If a family therapist never discusses the substance use itself, or intervenes around it,
that is NOT SUD treatment.

---

# 6. What makes a program SUD treatment vs not?

Here is the fundamental reality:

**SUD treatment is defined by CONTENT, not MODEL.**

Any model — CBT, MI, FFT, MDFT, etc. — is SUD treatment **ONLY IF**:

* they directly intervene on the youth’s drug-use behaviors
* they teach skills to reduce or replace drug use
* they identify and modify triggers
* they work on relapse prevention
* they change family/peer contexts that support the use
* they track substance-related outcomes

If they do NOT do those things,
they are **not** doing SUD treatment —
even if they are delivering a high-quality evidence-based model.

---

# 7. The ONE SENTENCE that is the answer to your entire question

### **SUD treatment is ordinary therapy methods applied directly and repeatedly to the youth’s substance use behavior, triggers, motivations, skills, and environment.**

That’s it.
That is the essence.
Everything else is detail.

---

# 8. So can FFT alone be SUD treatment?

### Yes — **if** FFT sessions include SUD-specific content.

### No — if FFT is only addressing general behavior or family conflict.

FFT is **NOT** inherently SUD treatment.
It becomes SUD treatment when:

* the therapist uses FFT techniques to alter substance use patterns
* the treatment plan lists substance use
* sessions address substance-use triggers, peer associations, refusal skills
* the family is taught monitoring, structure, and relapse-prevention strategies

Otherwise, it is just family therapy.

---

# 9. Here is your concrete checklist (finally):

### A provider is delivering **juvenile SUD treatment** if you can check ALL of these:

1. **They explicitly discuss the youth’s substance use in sessions**
2. **There are SUD-specific goals in the treatment plan**
3. **They teach skills directly related to drug use (refusal, coping, relapse management)**
4. **They address the youth’s drug-use triggers**
5. **They address family supervision and patterns tied to drug use**
6. **They measure SUD outcomes (UAs, triggers, self-report use)**
7. **They use a structured model while doing the above**

If any item is missing →
**It is NOT SUD treatment.**
